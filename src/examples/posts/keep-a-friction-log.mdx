By Thursday I had walked back to my desk for the same adapter three times. Each time I called myself forgetful. When I wrote down what had happened, the solution was right there. The adapter lived in the drawer. I needed it in the bag.

A friction log takes the snag out of the mood it arrives in and puts it somewhere you can see it. You only need one page and a week that looks reasonably like a week you’ll have again.

<MarginNote id="the-missing-adapter" label="The missing adapter">
  I almost opened a shopping tab to buy a second adapter. Moving the first one took five seconds. I left a small empty space in the bag so I could tell when it was missing.
</MarginNote>

## Catch the moment

For five working days, leave a scrap of paper near the place you do most of your work. Write down a line when something costs you a little attention. You waited, reopened a file, copied the same words, or went looking for an object that should have been within reach.

Use the same three prompts each time.

1. **What were you trying to do?** Send the updated sketch.
2. **Where did you get stuck?** Finding the export I made yesterday.
3. **How did you finish?** I exported a second copy and hoped I’d chosen the latest version.

Keep the adjectives for later. “The folder was a disaster” takes longer to write and gives you less to go on. “Two files called final” is a detail you can act on.

If you miss a day, carry on. The log works on whatever you caught in the moment. Reconstructing every annoyance from memory would make five days of ordinary work feel like a bad week.

<MarginNote id="the-snags-we-stop-seeing" label="The snags we stop seeing" kind="hunch">
  I wonder how many repeats never make it onto the page because I’ve started treating them as part of the job. Another person doing the same task might notice a completely different set.
</MarginNote>

## Look for repeats

At the end of the week, read the page through once. Circle entries that share a cause, even if they happened in different places. The wrong export and the missing notes might both trace back to the same question. Where does the current version live?

Pick one circle. A fix that takes ten minutes and saves you a small decision every day is a perfectly good result. Rename one folder, move one object, or write the sentence you keep having to compose. Give the change another week before deciding what to do next.

> Pay attention to the place where you keep inventing a workaround.

<MarginNote id="keep-the-good-friction" label="Keep one deliberate pause" href="/posts/who-decides-what-comes-next/" linkLabel="Read Who decides what comes next?">
  I kept the extra step before sending a proof to a client. It gives me a moment to check the recipient and the attached file. The essay on defaults looks at who gets to decide which pauses a tool leaves in place.
</MarginNote>

## Keep one deliberate pause

An extra step can earn its place. Reading a message one last time catches a brusque sentence. Copying a quotation by hand lets you hear its rhythm. Leave those entries on the page and see whether you value what happens inside the delay.

On my first Friday, I fixed the adapter and left two other circles for later. That left me enough room to spot something I’d missed. The most irritating entry was a question I’d been putting off asking a colleague. Once I asked it, a whole folder of workarounds fell out of use.

<MarginNote id="the-weekly-review" label="Keep the old page">
  Date the page and keep it for a month. If you test a fix and the same entry shows up in two weeks, you have a better starting point than “This still feels messy.”
</MarginNote>

Start wherever you are. The next time you reach for something that isn’t there, write down what you were reaching for.
