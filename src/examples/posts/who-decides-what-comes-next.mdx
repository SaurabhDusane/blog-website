I opened a blank presentation and got a deck of options. There was a client pitch, a quarterly review, and a photo-heavy travel report. All three looked finished enough to borrow. My actual task was to explain why a small project had become hard to continue, and I could feel myself turning that discussion into a pitch before I’d written a word.

Suggestions are handy when the model in front of you matches what you’re trying to do. The closer it looks to finished, the easier it is to mistake its next step for yours.

<MarginNote id="the-deck-of-options" label="The deck I almost made">
  The opening template put results on slide two. That might have been fine for a retrospective. I needed everyone to see the handoffs between four people before we got to what had happened. A sketch of the handoffs became the whole meeting.
</MarginNote>

## Look at the first suggestion

A template knows something about the job. It knows which parts recur and which titles feel familiar. Its blank spaces also suggest what kinds of material belong. Give someone a box marked “Key takeaway” before they’ve sorted their evidence, and you’ve invited them to decide what the evidence means.

The same thing happens in smaller ways all day. A document invites you to pick a tone for a sentence. A search box finishes a question with the phrasing someone else might use. A calendar makes a half-hour conversation the size of a default appointment. Each behavior represents a choice about what to make easy.

<SourceCitation source="do-defaults-save-lives" label="Read the research note on default choices" />

That ease can be generous. I welcome a stack of known formats when writing a receipt, and I’m glad when the calendar keeps the last room I used. The interesting moments are the ones where a tool offers me the satisfaction of a decision before I’ve made it.

<SourceCitation source="ftc-dark-patterns" label="Read the FTC source note on deceptive design" />

<MarginNote id="a-useful-default" label="A shared place to start" kind="objection">
  A blank page can make newcomers guess the rules everyone else already knows. A familiar template can give them an opening. I’d ask whether they can see its assumptions and change the order when the task calls for it.
</MarginNote>

## Ask what it assumes

Pick a tool you use often and look at one of its defaults. Pay attention to what it proposes before you supply much information. Try answering these questions on paper.

1. **What does the tool assume?** That I want to give a clean report on a completed project.
2. **What do I need to decide?** Which part of an ongoing project we can still change.
3. **What do I want to show first?** A sketch of the handoffs and the place work slows down.

If the answers line up, borrow the suggestion. If there’s a gap, try making the first move yourself. You can bring the tool back in when you know what you want it to carry.

<MarginNote id="defaults-have-authors" label="Defaults have authors">
  When you build a template for coworkers, write one sentence about what it assumes. “Use this when the group already agrees on the problem” can spare someone a surprising amount of revision.
</MarginNote>

> The choice worth keeping is the one that changes what you notice.

## Choose what to keep

I keep a short list of these choices for projects that feel hard to start. What would I put first if I had no headings? How much space would I leave for someone to reply? What could I show in one image before putting anything into prose?

For that meeting, I brought the sketch and one short question. A teammate noticed a handoff I had left off entirely. They drew it on the whiteboard, and we spent the rest of the meeting talking about who was waiting on it. By the end I had more to put in a report than I’d had when I was looking at the report template.

<MarginNote id="make-a-shortlist" label="Make a short list" href="/posts/the-case-for-a-rough-draft/" linkLabel="Read The case for a rough draft">
  Making an early version by hand can be a low-effort way to find the first choice you want to own. A file with three rough lines is enough to take back to your usual tool.
</MarginNote>

There’s room in the work for defaults you chose and defaults you inherited. When a new project asks you to begin, try noticing which kind you reach for first.
