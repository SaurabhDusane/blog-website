Last week I opened a draft called “Notes on a Better Morning” and spent twenty minutes moving the title around. The file contained a title and one sentence. Eventually I typed, “I keep starting the day by checking what everyone else did with theirs.” I had a subject at last. The title could wait.

The first version of an idea has a small job. It needs to give you something to look at. That might be a paragraph you half believe, a sketch with the dimensions wrong, or a recording you’d only play to yourself. Once the thing exists, you can ask more useful questions of it.

<MarginNote id="save-the-first-line" label="Save the first line" kind="observation">
  The line about mornings came out of a list of things I did before opening that file. The early title disappeared. I kept the list beside the finished piece, and the last detail on it became the ending.
</MarginNote>

## Give the draft a small job

On a team project, I once sketched a dashboard as three rectangles on index cards. I wanted to ask which number a person should see first. Moving the cards took a few seconds. If I’d brought a polished screen, I suspect we’d have spent the meeting on the shade of blue.

Writing has an equivalent. Start with one scene, one claim, or one actual example. Read it aloud, then write down the first question you ask yourself. A contradiction counts as a good result. It tells you where to spend the next twenty minutes.

> A rough draft gives you evidence about your own idea.

Try keeping a line at the top of the file that begins “I’m trying to find out whether…” Finish it in ordinary language. “Whether the meeting was too long” gives you something to investigate. “Whether the structure of our meetings serves our strategic goals” gives you a paragraph to spend the afternoon staring at.

## Leave something to grab

When I ask someone to read an early draft, I send one question. Where did you need an example? What would you try first? Who do you think this is for? A reader can answer any of those in the time it takes to make tea. “Thoughts?” asks them to invent both the review and its terms.

The part that feels too loose to share is sometimes the part that makes a conversation possible. Put brackets around it and say what you’re still deciding. A reader may offer a detail you forgot or a better order for the pieces you already have.

<MarginNote id="leave-some-tooth" label="Leave some tooth">
  In this draft, the short question about meetings started as a note to myself in brackets. A reader put a check mark beside it. The paragraph I thought needed the least work turned out to be the easy one to cut.
</MarginNote>

## Decide when to revise

Even a generous rough draft needs a boundary. I give mine one work session, then jot down the next action before closing the file. “Find the receipt with the date” or “ask Jo which example made sense” is enough. There’s something specific to return to tomorrow.

Sometimes a project stalls because its next action depends on a decision no one has made. The draft can make that visible. Circle the place you keep rewriting and name the unresolved choice beside it. You may find the most useful revision starts with a conversation.

<MarginNote id="ask-a-smaller-question" label="Ask a smaller question" href="/posts/keep-a-friction-log/" linkLabel="Read Keep a friction log">
  If you keep circling the same place, log what happens just before you reach it. The repeat is a useful clue. The next essay includes a small experiment for catching it while it’s happening.
</MarginNote>

For your next idea, open a blank file and set a timer for twelve minutes. Give the version you make a name with a date on it. You can return tomorrow and see exactly what you had to work with.
